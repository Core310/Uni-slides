Note:
Today we are breaking down Judith Butler's 1993 essay Critically Queer, which is the final chapter of Bodies That Matter (pages 223 to 242). Butler is often treated like an impossible philosopher, but their core mechanism is very direct once you strip away the dense jargon: how language materializes bodies into men and women, how compulsory heterosexuality is enforced through hidden grief, and how marginalized groups can take a weaponized slur like queer and turn it into a tool of political defiance. Over the next fifteen minutes, we will walk through Butler's text section by section, clear up the biggest misunderstandings about performativity, connect the reading to Paris is Burning, and end with three discussion questions for the class.

## Opening the Text: Affirming "Queer" (pp. 223–225)
- The Urgent Question: What does it mean to affirm a term that has historically functioned as a lethal street slur?
- The Historical Crisis (1993): Devastating AIDS death toll met by federal silence and institutional abandonment
- Militant Direct Action: ACT UP (1987) and Queer Nation (1990) reject polite assimilationist lobbying
- The Strategic Paradox: How to mobilize "queer" for collective resistance without freezing it into a new exclusionary category

Note:
Butler opens Critically Queer on page 223 by asking a direct political question: what actually happens when an oppressed community seizes the very slur used against them and uses it as a badge of honor? To understand why Butler wrote this in 1993, you have to look at the atmosphere of the early nineties. The HIV/AIDS epidemic was wiping out an entire generation of gay men, trans women, and people of color, while the government and medical establishment met the catastrophe with silence and moral condemnation. In response, activist groups like ACT UP and Queer Nation broke away from polite gay rights groups that were trying to appeal to middle-class respectability. Instead of asking for tolerance, they took over streets, staged die-ins, and weaponized the word queer. Butler writes this essay directly in the shadow of that movement, analyzing both the radical power and the inherent dangers of organizing around a reclaimed name.

## Clearing the Myth: Performance vs. Performativity (pp. 224–226)
- The Misreading of Gender Trouble (1990): Critics assumed gender was a voluntary theatrical costume picked out of a wardrobe
- Butler's Correction in Bodies That Matter: "Performativity is neither free play nor theatrical self-presentation"
- Performance: A conscious, theatrical act staged by an already-existing subject
- Performativity: The forced, ritualized repetition through which a subject is produced in the first place

Note:
On pages 224 to 226, Butler addresses the single most widespread misunderstanding of their work: confusing theatrical performance with performativity. When Butler published Gender Trouble three years earlier, readers celebrated them because they thought Butler was saying, 'Gender is just a show! You can put on high heels today and combat boots tomorrow, and change your gender whenever you feel like it.' Butler explicitly rejects that voluntaristic reading in Bodies That Matter. You are not an already-existing sovereign individual who gets to pick your gender out of a wardrobe. Rather, performativity is a compulsory, disciplinary script forced upon you by legal, medical, and social institutions. You do not do gender; gender does you. It is a set of expectations and punishments that shape your body long before you ever develop conscious thought.

## The Linguistic Engine: J.L. Austin & "Girling" (pp. 226–227)

> "In that naming, the girl is 'girled', brought into the domain of language and kinship through the interpellation of gender." — Judith Butler, Bodies That Matter (1993, p. 232)

- J.L. Austin (1962): Ordinary Language Philosophy and "Performative Utterances"
- Descriptive vs. Performative: Constative words describe facts; performative utterances accomplish an action
- The Medical Interpellation: A doctor declaring "It is a girl!" at birth is not reporting a biological fact
- "Girling" as Compulsion: Initiating an administrative, legal, and disciplinary pipeline of gendered surveillance

Note:
Where does Butler get the word performativity? On pages 226 and 227, Butler draws directly from ordinary language philosopher J.L. Austin and his 1962 book How to Do Things With Words. Austin pointed out that language does not just describe the world; certain words actually do things. When a judge says 'I sentence you to five years,' or an officiant says 'I pronounce you husband and wife,' speaking the words creates a new social reality. Read the quote on screen: 'In that naming, the girl is girled, brought into the domain of language and kinship.' In plain English, what Butler is saying is that the doctor at birth is not reporting a biological discovery. It is an administrative assignment. It initiates what Butler calls the lifelong process of girling, placing the infant into a regulatory pipeline of schools, families, and laws.

## Derrida & Citationality: Fragility of the Script (pp. 227–228)

> "This repetition is not performed by a subject; this repetition is what enables a subject and constitutes the temporal condition for the subject." — Judith Butler (1993, p. 231)

- Jacques Derrida's Iterability: Words only hold authority by citing prior historical conventions
- No Natural Original: Gender scripts carry force only because they echo established social precedent
- Compulsory Repetition: We do not invent gender; we are forced to repeatedly cite pre-existing historical norms
- The Structural Glitch: Because repetition is never exact, every citation introduces the risk of slippage, failure, and subversion

Note:
Next, on pages 227 and 228, Butler uses Jacques Derrida's critique of Austin. Why does a judge's sentence or an officiant's wedding decree have power? Derrida argues it only works because it cites an established historical law. If an actor on a Broadway stage says 'I sentence you to prison,' nobody goes to jail, because the context does not cite real state authority. Look at the quote on screen: 'This repetition is not performed by a subject; this repetition is what enables a subject.' Butler means you do not choose your gender script; repeating the script is what allows society to recognize you as a human subject in the first place. But here is the punchline: because gender must be repeated every single day, the system is fragile. Every repetition introduces the chance of an error, a glitch, or a subversion. Normative heterosexuality is terrified of these slippages because they reveal that the entire system is an artificial construction, not a law of nature.

## Discursive Resignification: Inverting the Slur (pp. 228–230)
- The Wound of the Name: Being hailed by a slur cites centuries of state violence, police raids, and physical abuse
- The Mechanics of Resignification: Citing the derogatory term in an unintended, non-authorized context
- Turning Trauma into Coalition: How ACT UP and Queer Nation detached "queer" from homophobic violence and made it a battle cry
- A Permanent Paradox: The word always retains the ghost of its violent past, which is precisely where its political shock value comes from

Note:
Now we get to the core of Critically Queer on pages 228 to 230: linguistic resignification. Butler asks, how does a community fight back against speech that wounds? When someone hurls a homophobic slur at you on the street, the injury does not come from the acoustic sound of the word. It hurts because that word cites centuries of state violence, police raids, and physical abuse. But Butler points out that language has no fixed, permanent owner. Because words rely on repetition, a targeted community can grab that slur, pull it out of its original violent context, and cite it back against the dominant culture. When thousands of activists march down the street yelling 'We're here, we're queer,' they are not erasing the history of the word; they are exploiting its shock value. Resignification works because the trauma of the word is turned inside out to expose the violence of the oppressor.

## Heterosexual Melancholia & Disavowed Loss (pp. 232–236)

> "The straight man becomes the man he cannot love, and the straight woman becomes the woman she cannot love." — Judith Butler (1993, p. 235)

- The Heterosexual Matrix: Instituted through the compulsory prohibition of same-sex love
- Disavowed Grief: To become a "proper" straight man, you must violently renounce attachments to men, but society forbids you from mourning that loss
- Social Melancholia: Straight identity is built upon an internal graveyard of unacknowledged, unmourned queer attachments
- The Psychoanalytic Cost: Gender identification is not natural love; it is the melancholic internalization of a forbidden tie

Note:
On pages 232 to 236, Butler develops one of the deepest psychoanalytic arguments in the text: heterosexual melancholia. Look at this famous quote on the screen: 'The straight man becomes the man he cannot love, and the straight woman becomes the woman she cannot love.' Butler asks what it actually costs a person to fit into normal heterosexuality. To become a conventional man, you do not just learn to love women; you are forced to violently renounce any love for other men, and you are strictly forbidden from mourning that loss. If a straight man mourns the loss of another man, his own straightness is questioned. Butler argues that normative straightness is literally built on top of an internal graveyard of unacknowledged queer attachments.

## The Politics of Mourning & Ungrievable AIDS Lives (pp. 236–239)
- The 1993 Crisis Grounding: Moving Freud's theory of melancholia into the literal trauma of the AIDS epidemic
- Ungrievable Lives: Mainstream American culture refused to publicly acknowledge or mourn the deaths of thousands of gay men
- Dehumanization via Silence: By treating queer deaths as unworthy of national grief, the state strips queer bodies of human status
- Militant Mourning as Resistance: ACT UP's political funerals and "ashes actions" turn private grief into public political fury

Note:
On pages 236 to 239, Butler shifts Freud's concept of melancholia directly into the politics of the AIDS epidemic. This was not an abstract classroom debate in 1993. Tens of thousands of gay men were dying, and the federal government refused to say the word AIDS or hold national moments of mourning. Butler shows that refusing to grieve someone is the ultimate act of dehumanization. If your death cannot be mourned, your life was never considered a real human life. In response, activist groups like ACT UP weaponized grief. They held political funerals in the middle of Wall Street, and scattered the actual cremated ashes of their lovers onto the White House lawn. Butler points out that when the state forbids you from mourning, public grief becomes the most militant political act you have.

## Butler's Warning: Keeping "Queer" Contested (pp. 239–242)

> "If the term 'queer' is to remain a site of contestation, it must not become the property of an elite, nor can it become a term of exclusion." — Judith Butler (1993, p. 230)

- Against Gatekeeping: "Queer" must never become a closed membership card or a fixed demographic checklist
- The Anti-Normative Edge: The moment an identity category becomes respectable, it starts policing its own borders
- A Site of Permanent Contestation: Butler insists the term must remain unfinished, fragile, and vulnerable to new challenges
- Core Conclusion: Language is both a cage and a lever; power disciplines us, but power's reliance on repetition gives us the chance to subvert it

Note:
Butler concludes Critically Queer on pages 239 to 242 with a crucial warning: the term queer must never settle down. Look at the quote on screen: 'If the term queer is to remain a site of contestation, it must not become the property of an elite, nor can it become a term of exclusion.' Butler warns that if the movement turns queer into a standardized checklist of who qualifies and who does not, it will end up policing people just like the heterosexual matrix did. The moment a radical word becomes respectable, corporate, or institutionalized, it loses its ability to challenge power. For Butler, the political strength of queer lies in the fact that it is unstable, unfinished, and always contested.

## Course Connection: Paris is Burning & "Realness"

> "At the balls, the white corporate executive is not mocked; he is worshipped as the supreme fantasy of power and safety." — bell hooks, Black Looks (1992)

- Harlem Drag Ball Culture: Competing in categories like "Executive Realness", "Town and Country", and "Military"
- The Illusion of the Original: Ball culture reveals that dominant straight corporate identity is also an imitation without an original
- Realness as Survival: Passing is not just fun theater; for poor trans women of color (like Venus Xtravaganza), failing to pass meant death
- Subversion vs. Re-inscription: Does copying white, wealthy corporate aesthetics challenge dominant power or reinforce it?

Note:
In our course syllabus, Butler is paired directly with Jennie Livingston's 1990 documentary Paris is Burning. In the balls, contestants compete for realness—blending in completely as a white corporate executive or wealthy housewife. Butler argues that realness proves all gender is an imitation without an original. But notice the counter-perspective from bell hooks on the slide: 'At the balls, the white corporate executive is not mocked; he is worshipped as the supreme fantasy of power and safety.' Hooks reminds us that ball contestants are not just playing with academic gender theory; they are poor, homeless, Black and Latino queer folks worshipping the only forms of power that offer physical safety. When Venus Xtravaganza was murdered, it proved that failing to achieve realness carries lethal consequences.

## The Exclusions: Trans, Race, Asexuality & Global Politics

> "Transsexuality is not a metaphor; it is the material struggle to inhabit a body that feels liveable." — Jay Prosser, Second Skins (1998)

- Transgender Critique (Jay Prosser & Viviane Namaste): Challenged Butler for reducing trans embodiment and transition to an ironic linguistic metaphor for cis deconstruction, rather than recognizing bodily dysphoria and medical survival
- Race, Class & the White Gaze (bell hooks): Criticized white theorists for romanticizing Harlem drag balls as radical rebellion while ignoring the racial capitalism and street violence killing Black and Latina trans women
- Asexuality & Allonormativity: 1990s queer theory operated under an allonormative assumption that liberation required celebrating erotic transgression, erasing asexual, aromantic, and celibate lives
- Global & Postcolonial Limits (Jasbir Puar & Joseph Massad): "Queer" is an Anglo-American linguistic product; exporting it globally risks overwriting non-Western indigenous gender systems under the guise of universal Western human rights

Note:
To critically evaluate Butler from a global politics perspective, we have to ask who was excluded from early 1990s queer theory. Look at Jay Prosser's quote on screen: 'Transsexuality is not a metaphor; it is the material struggle to inhabit a body that feels liveable.' Prosser and Viviane Namaste pushed back hard against Butler for treating trans people as convenient theoretical props. Butler framed drag as proof that gender is an illusion, but for many trans people, transitioning is not an ironic parody; it is a literal struggle for bodily coherence, medical survival, and legal recognition. Second, on race and class: bell hooks pointed out that white academics romanticized Paris is Burning while ignoring the racial capitalism that killed women like Venus Xtravaganza. Third, early queer theory was hyper-sexualized, completely erasing asexual people. Finally, in global politics, exporting the American word queer globally risks overriding indigenous gender systems.

## Three Class Discussion Questions
1. Performativity in Daily Life: Butler argues that gender is an enforced repetition of norms rather than an outfit chosen each morning. Where in university life, sports, or family structures do you see gender enforced through disciplinary repetition rather than personal choice?
2. Limits of Resignification: Given how the word "queer" was resignified from a lethal slur into an academic department and political banner, are there limits to linguistic resignification? Can any derogatory term be completely stripped of its trauma, or does the original wound always linger?
3. Realness & Exclusion: In *Paris is Burning*, contestants achieve "realness" by imitating white corporate and military power. Connecting to bell hooks and Jay Prosser, does achieving realness challenge dominant hierarchies, or does it risk reinforcing white wealth and cisnormativity as the only standard of survival?

Note:
To wrap up our fifteen minutes, I have three questions to throw out to the room:
First, thinking about Butler's claim that gender is a disciplinary routine rather than a personal choice, where on campus or in your personal routines do you experience gender as a mandatory rule rather than an expression of personal freedom?
Second, on linguistic resignification: we saw how the word queer was flipped from a violent insult into an empowering coalition banner. But does that work for all slurs? Can a community ever completely erase the trauma of an injurious word, or does the history of violence always leave a mark?
Third, looking at Paris is Burning and the critiques from bell hooks and Jay Prosser: when ball contestants walk the floor to prove they can pass as white Wall Street executives or military officers, are they subverting the system by proving anyone can perform it, or are they re-affirming that elite white wealth is the only status that truly matters?
